Field notes from the shoreline. Some mornings the sea hands you everything at once: light, glass, a set that stacks up exactly where you hoped it would. Most mornings it makes you wait. The waiting is the part nobody photographs, which is a shame, because that is where you learn to read the water.

Keep scrolling. Every image on this page carries the same .imr-reveal class, and the browser does the rest.

Reading the water

Swell arrives in families. Count the seconds between crests and you know how far the storm that sent them has travelled; watch where the foam folds first and you know what the bottom is doing underneath. None of it is mystical. It is just attention, applied daily, until the sea becomes legible.

The reveal works the same way. The element's own journey through the viewport is the timeline. There is no duration, no delay, no easing curve fighting your scroll speed. You scrub the animation with your thumb, and if you scroll back up, the curtain politely closes again.

Between tides

Slack water is the quiet hinge between ebb and flood, ten or twenty minutes when the sea seems to hold its breath. Fishermen love it, photographers love it more. Everything goes still and reflective, and colors you never see at noon float up to the surface.

Under the hood this whole effect is one keyframe rule and one timeline binding. The animation range, entry 25% cover 50%, means the unveiling starts once a quarter of the image has entered and completes as it reaches the middle of your screen. Two lines to tune, nothing to configure.

Mapping the reef

Every home break gets a hand-drawn map eventually. Not an accurate one, a useful one: where the channel runs, which coral heads bite at low tide, the lineup marks you triangulate from the beach. It lives on the back of an envelope and it is worth more than any chart.

Consider this part the same kind of map. Copy the stylesheet, drop the class on your images, and adjust two custom properties until the reveal feels like yours. Browsers that do not speak view timelines yet simply show the finished picture, and readers who prefer reduced motion never see the curtain at all.
